Mechanics Modelling Assumptions

Every A-level mechanics question quietly says "light", "smooth", "uniform" or "ignore air resistance". Switch each assumption off and see what the model was hiding.

Model: assumption madeReality: assumption removed

Projectile model

Air resistance is negligible

The ball is a particle with gravity as the only force. The horizontal velocity never changes, so R = u² sin 2θ / g and the path is a symmetric parabola.

Remove the assumption and drag acts against the velocity. Here it is modelled as cv² per unit mass, so it opposes both the horizontal and the vertical motion.

ModelReality

Ask the class. Which of the five SUVAT equations stops working once drag is included, and why?

When is it fair? A shot put loses under 1% of its range. A football or cricket ball loses over a quarter at match speeds.

Shading shows where the mass is concentrated. G is the centre of mass, M is the midpoint.
Rods, beams and ladders

The body is uniform

A uniform beam has its weight acting at the midpoint, so we can place W in the centre and use symmetry. On two supports each carries half the weight.

A real plank, tapered pole or loaded ladder is heavier at one end. Its centre of mass G moves, and moments about each support no longer cancel.

ModelReality

Ask the class. Beam length 4 m, mass 20 kg, supports 0.5 m from each end. How far from the left end can G be before the beam tips?

When is it fair? A plank cut from one piece of timber, a metal bar, a ruler. Not a ladder with a person on it.

Dashed outline: where the model puts each mass.
Connected particles

The string is inextensible

An inextensible string keeps both masses moving together at the same speed. The tension is one steady value, T = 2m1m2g / (m1 + m2).

A real string stretches, like a spring with modulus k. Released from rest it is first slack in effect, then overshoots: the tension swings from 0 up to twice the model value.

ModelReality

Ask the class. Why is a stiff rope still "inextensible" even though it stretches a little?

When is it fair? When the stretch is tiny against the distances moved. A nylon cord stretches about a millimetre here, far too small to see.

Surfaces

The surface is smooth

With no friction the only force along a slope is the weight component, so a = g sin θ. On the flat, a moving block keeps its speed for ever.

With friction, F ≤ μR acts against the motion. Down a slope a = g(sin θ − μ cos θ), and below a critical angle the block does not move at all.

SmoothRough

Ask the class. The mass cancels in both answers. Why does it still matter for the size of the friction force?

When is it fair? Ice, a lubricated track or an air table. Not rubber on tarmac, where μ is near 1.

Pulleys and pegs

The pulley is smooth

A smooth pulley changes the direction of the string without changing the tension, so T1 = T2 and one equation covers the whole string.

Friction at the axle resists the turning. The tensions differ by the friction force, T1 − T2 = f, and a small difference in mass may not be enough to start any motion.

ModelReality

Ask the class. Set the masses to 2.5 kg and 2.0 kg, then slide the friction up. At what value does the system stop moving?

When is it fair? A well-oiled bearing on a light wheel. Not a rope dragged over a rough fixed peg.
