Modelling in Mechanics: Newton's Laws

A-Level Mechanics · Newton's laws · take g = 9.8 m/s²

After the collision, how fast is the blue ball moving?

Newton's third law: if two bodies exert forces on each other, the forces are equal in size and opposite in direction.

Modelling assumptions

  • The balls are particlesWe ignore their size and any spin. Each ball is just a point with a mass.
  • The surface is smoothThere is no friction to slow the balls down before or after the hit.
  • No air resistanceNothing else pushes on the balls while they move.
  • The collision is directBoth balls move along the same straight line, before and after the hit.
  • No outside forces during the hitOnly the two balls push on each other, so total momentum is conserved.
  • The masses do not changeNothing breaks off either ball in the collision.

These assumptions make the maths simpler. The answer is a good model of real life, but not a perfect one.

Question: The two spheres start as shown. After they collide the yellow ball is stationary. What speed must the blue ball be travelling at?

Have a go in your book first, then check your answer.

  1. During the hit, the balls push on each other with equal and opposite forces (third law), for the same length of time.

  2. So the momentum the yellow ball loses is exactly the momentum the blue ball gains. Total momentum is conserved.
    momentum = mass × velocity

  3. Before the collision:
    1 × 10 + 5 × 0 = 10 kg m/s

  4. After the collision (yellow stops, blue moves at v):
    1 × 0 + 5 × v = 5v

  5. Before = after:
    5v = 10  ⇒  v = 2

Answer2 m/sto the right
Why so slow? The blue ball has 5 times the mass, so the same momentum gives it only one fifth of the speed: 10 ÷ 5 = 2.

What forces act on a 4 kg weight sitting on a table?

Newton's third law: if two bodies exert forces on each other, the forces are equal in size and opposite in direction.

Modelling assumptions

  • The block is a particleWe draw all forces acting at one point, ignoring its size and shape.
  • The table is rigid and horizontalIt does not bend or slope, so the reaction acts straight up.
  • The block is at restIt is in equilibrium, so the forces on it balance.
  • g = 9.8 m/s²Gravity is the same everywhere near the Earth's surface.
  • No other forcesNo wind, no one pushing it, and no air resistance.

These assumptions make the maths simpler. The answer is a good model of real life, but not a perfect one.

Question: A 4 kg weight sits on a wooden table. Describe the forces acting on the object.

Have a go in your book first, then check your answer.

  1. Weight pulls the block down. It is caused by gravity.
    W = mg = 4 × 9.8 = 39.2 N

  2. The block is not moving, so the forces on it must balance. The table pushes up with the normal reaction R.
    R = W = 39.2 N

  3. Third law: the block pushes down on the table with 39.2 N, so the table pushes up on the block with 39.2 N. Tap the button to see this pair.

AnswerW = 39.2 N down, R = 39.2 N up
Common mistake: W and R are not a third-law pair. They act on the same object, and they are different kinds of force. A third-law pair acts on two different objects. The partner of the weight is the block pulling the Earth upwards by 39.2 N.

What thrust does the rocket produce?

Newton's second law: the resultant force on a body equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration. F = ma

Modelling assumptions

  • The rocket is a particleWe ignore its size and shape and treat it as a point.
  • The mass stays at 800 kgA real rocket gets lighter as it burns fuel. We ignore this.
  • No gravityIn deep space it is far from any planet or star, so it has no weight.
  • No air resistanceSpace is a vacuum, so there is nothing to push against it.
  • Thrust is constantThe engine pushes steadily in the direction of motion, so the acceleration is constant.

These assumptions make the maths simpler. The answer is a good model of real life, but not a perfect one.

Every second the rocket gets 16 m/s faster. That steady increase is what an acceleration of 16 m/s² means.

Question: What is the thrust (force) generated by this rocket? Note: it is in deep space.

Have a go in your book first, then check your answer.

  1. In deep space there is no gravity and no air resistance, so the thrust is the only force. It is the resultant force.

  2. Use Newton's second law:
    F = ma

  3. Put in the numbers:
    F = 800 × 16 = 12 800

Answer12 800 N(12.8 kN)
Why say "deep space"? Near the Earth, weight would also act on the rocket. Then F = ma would give the resultant force, and the thrust would have to be bigger to make up for gravity.

Why does a thrown ball follow a curved path?

Newton's first law: a body stays at rest, or moves at constant speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it.

Modelling assumptions

  • The ball is a particleWe ignore its size and any spin on it.
  • No air resistanceAfter it leaves your hand, the only force on the ball is its weight.
  • Gravity is constantg = 9.8 m/s² straight down, the same at every height the ball reaches.
  • The ground is flatThe Earth's curve and spin are too small to matter here.
  • Nothing else touches itNo wind or other objects push on the ball in flight.

These assumptions make the maths simpler. The answer is a good model of real life, but not a perfect one.

Question: When a ball is thrown on Earth it travels in a parabolic curve. Explain this using Newton's first law.

Have a go in your book first, then check your answer.

  1. If no force acted, the first law says the ball would carry on in a straight line at constant speed (the faint line).

  2. But on Earth a force does act: the ball's weight, pulling straight down all the time.

  3. Sideways: there is no force (ignoring air resistance), so by the first law the sideways speed stays the same.

  4. Up and down: the weight changes the vertical speed. The ball slows as it rises, stops at the top, then speeds up as it falls.

  5. Steady sideways motion plus changing vertical motion bends the path into a parabola.

AnswerWeight acts, so the path can't stay straight
Key idea: a force is needed to change motion, not to keep it going. Nothing pushes the ball forward after it leaves your hand.
